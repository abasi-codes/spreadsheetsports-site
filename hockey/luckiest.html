Luckiest model · all-time

Who's the luckiest
NHL player ever?

Henri Richard. He won 11 Stanley Cups, the most ever, and my model says his own play earned about 1.6 of them. His teammates alone would have been expected to win about 6.2. I scored 2,163 finished NHL careers and ran the model 10,000 different ways: he finished first in 35.8% of them, about twice as often as anyone else.

The answer

The case for Henri Richard

A Hall of Fame centre who played all 20 of his seasons for the Canadiens. He won the Cup in each of his first five, and no player in history had stronger teammates for longer.

Stanley Cups
11

The most by any player. 20 seasons, all with Montreal, 1955 to 1975.

Cups his own play earned
1.6

His team's Cup odds with him, minus its odds with his own point shares taken off the standings.

Cups his teammates alone would win
6.2

The most of anyone in the 2,163 careers. His Canadiens were good enough to win without him.

Cups expected
7.8

At his teams' real odds, also the most in the pool. He still won 3.2 more than that.

Favourites knocked out for him
7

In 7 of his 11 Cup years, the pre-playoff favourite was beaten by somebody else before Montreal had to face it.

Playoff bounce goals
+65

Goals his teams gained from hot shooting and hot goaltending in the playoffs. He also went 3-1 in series that went the distance.

How the model works

The pool

Every finished NHL career with at least 5 full seasons of games (goalies 4), 1918 to 2026. 2,163 careers; 807 won at least one Cup. Active players are left out, because an unfinished career can't be scored.

The team Cup model

Each playoff team gets a fair share of that year's Cup from its regular-season points percentage (a conditional logit, the same model as my McDavid and Unluckiest videos).

Version 1 · Cups per point share

Cups divided by career point shares. It rewards being bad: the leader won 4 Cups with fewer than 0 point shares. Leader: Kevin McClelland.

Version 2 · Cups he didn't earn

His team's Cup odds with him, minus its odds with his own point shares taken off its standings points, is the part he earned. The rest is luck. Leader: Henri Richard.

Version 3 · Dragons

The pre-playoff favourite knocked out by somebody else in a year he won the Cup. Leader: Henri Richard.

Version 4 · Coin flips and bounces

Series that went the distance (double if the last game was decided by one goal), plus playoff goals gained because his team's shooters and goalies ran hot. Leader: Henri Richard.

Version 5 · Arrival and soft awards

Cups in a player's first or second season with a team, and Hart, Norris or Vezina wins in seasons someone else at his position had more point shares. Leader: Henri Richard.

10,000 universes

Each one randomly picks the weights, the player pool, the team ratings, the scaling, whether the 6-team era is discounted, whether teammates count as luck, and whether a Cup counts when he never played in that spring's playoffs. Richard finished first in 35.8%, ahead of Bryan Trottier (18.7%), Yvan Cournoyer (17.9%) and Jacques Lemaire (11.6%).

The backtest

A backtest means I hid one season at a time, trained the model on the other 98 and asked it to call the hidden season, like an old exam with the answers covered up. I did that 99 times.

Teams it gave about a 12% chance won 11.7% of the time (12 of 103). Across all 2,163 careers it expected 1,622 Cups, and they won 1,639.

Luck doesn't repeat

Split every career into odd and even seasons and score each half on its own. Points per game match between the halves (r = 0.95). Cups vs expected (0.19), bounces (0.09) and coin flips (0.07) barely do. That's what luck looks like. Teammates repeat (0.84), because they're circumstance, not chance, which is why some versions leave them out.

Caveats
  • Two judgement calls decide the order at the top: whether teammates count as luck, and whether a Cup counts when he was hurt and missed that whole playoff run (Cournoyer in 1977 and 1979). Both are split 50/50 across the universes.
  • Career totals favour long careers on dynasties. Per season, the answer is Ken Dryden.
  • The team model only sees the regular season, so it can't see deadline trades, and it slightly underrates strong favourites.
  • Bounce goals need shot data, which starts in 1959-60. That slightly hurts Richard, whose first Cups came earlier.

Sources: point shares from Hockey-Reference; games, playoff series, Cup rosters and awards from the NHL stats and records APIs.
