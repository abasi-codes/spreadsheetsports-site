The answer

The case for Brad Park

A Hall of Fame defenceman for the Rangers and the Bruins, from 1968 to 1985. He was in the room every spring and watched the door close.

Stanley Cups
0

In 17 seasons, and his team made the playoffs in every single one of them.

Cups expected
1.84

At his teams' real odds, he wins at least one Cup in 8,634 of 10,000 simulated careers. That's an 86% chance.

Seasons ended by the champion
9

His season was ended by the team that went on to win the Cup 9 times. The pool average is about 3.

Stanley Cup Finals lost
3

1972 vs Bruins, 1977 vs Canadiens, 1978 vs Canadiens.

Norris runner-ups
6

Four times behind Bobby Orr and twice behind Denis Potvin. He never won it.

Deciding games lost by one goal
4

Including the 1974 Game 7 against the Flyers and the 1979 too-many-men Game 7 in Montreal. His teams also lost 40 playoff goals to cold shooting and cold goaltending.

His best chance in any one season was only 20% (1971), about 1 in 5. But he got 17 tries.

10,000 universes

Change every choice, same answer

I chose the weights, the prime window and the pool. So I ran 10,000 versions with random weights, different pools and different team ratings, and sometimes deleted a whole factor.

Greatness × luck

I also tried a version where bad luck counts more the greater the player was. Park still came first in 54.5% of universes, and the only player who got close was Ray Bourque at 21.9%. In the main model, Bourque is #2 on the full board and finished top 5 in 36.1% of universes.

Method

How the model works

Version 1 · Great, no Cup

The simplest definition of bad luck: you were great, and you never won the Cup. Career point shares count only if the Cup count is zero. Leader: Roberto Luongo.

Version 2 · Cups expected vs won

Every season, his team gets its fair share of that year's Cup based on how good it was in the regular season. Expected Cups minus Cups won. Leader: Roberto Luongo.

Version 3 · Bodies

How much of his prime, age 22 to 31, he actually got to play. The average player in the pool lost about 9%. Leader: Cam Neely.

Version 4 · The dynasty tax

Every season that ended against the eventual champion, with extra weight for dynasties and for losing in the Final. Leader: Brad Park.

Version 5 · Bounces

Series lost in the last possible game (double if by one goal), plus playoff goals lost because his team's shooters and goalies went cold. Leader: Brad Park.

Version 6 · Circumstances

Weak teammates, 2nd-place award finishes, and bad timing: a team that just got rid of him wins the Cup. Leader: Brad Park.

10,000 universes

Every version of the model with random weights, pools, prime windows and team ratings. Park finished first in 79.5% and in the top 5 in 98.5%.

The backtest

Can it tell who should have won?

A backtest means I hid one season at a time and asked the model to call it from the final standings, like an old exam with the answers covered up. I did that for all 99 seasons.

When it gave a team about a 12% chance, those teams won 11.7% of the time (12 of 103). It expected 97 of the 305 players to finish without a Cup, and the real number is 96.

The pool: Hockey-Reference's top 250 careers in point shares plus every Hall of Famer with 300+ NHL games, with at least half the career after 1926. Active players are left out, because an unfinished career can't be scored. Goalies are included.

The twist

Jean Ratelle kept showing up near the top too (10th on the full board), and he was traded to Boston in the same 1975 deal as Park. They lost the same 3 Finals and 8 of the same 9 exits to the champion. In that 1979 Game 7, Ratelle had 3 assists.
